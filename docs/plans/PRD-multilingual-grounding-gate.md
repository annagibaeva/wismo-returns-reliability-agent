## 1. Summary

A customer support agent answers return requests by looking up rules in a written policy. A checking step called the grounding gate stops it from sending answers it cannot justify, and that check drove wrong answers to zero in English.

This project keeps the policy in English and gives the agent customers writing in Spanish and Indonesian. It measures whether the check still protects the customer, or only makes wrong answers look justified.

## 3. Background

### What exists today

The agent handles two kinds of support ticket. "Where is my order" is a simple lookup. "Can I return this" is a policy decision, and that is where mistakes are costly.

The return rules are stored as data rather than prose. Each rule has an ID, a condition that can be tested, and an answer it allows. After the agent proposes an answer, the grounding gate re-tests it against those rules. If the check fails, the answer is never sent and a human takes over.

Turning the gate on cut wrong answers from 10% to zero, while the agent kept resolving the same share of tickets it could handle.

### Why now

Three reasons.

**The English result is finished and the obvious next question is unanswered.** The gate works when the customer and the policy share a language. Nobody has measured what happens when they do not.

**Reading the code suggests the assumption is wrong.** The gate never sees the customer's message. It only sees structured data. That means it cannot notice when the system has misunderstood the customer, and it will approve an answer built on a misunderstanding.

**Reading a customer's own language is now practical.** Until recently, handling another language meant translating everything into English first. A model can now read Spanish or Indonesian directly and pull out what it needs. That makes the two approaches genuine alternatives, so comparing them produces a usable answer rather than a foregone one.

## 4. Objective

### What we are trying to find out

When the policy is written in English and the customer writes in something else, does the grounding gate protect the customer, or only protect the appearance of a justified answer?

### Why it matters

Support teams in most large companies keep one policy document and serve customers in many languages. Those teams reasonably assume that a system which checks its answers against written policy is safe in any language, because the check looks at rules rather than words.

If that assumption is wrong, nothing in the existing reporting flags it. The customer gets a wrong answer that quotes a real rule and leaves a clean record behind it. The dashboards stay green.

### Key results

| # | Result | Measure | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| KR1 | Every language has a pass or fail against all five quality conditions | Report generated by the test harness | 3 of 3 languages measured |
| KR2 | The invisible failure has a number attached | Silent fact error rate, per language | Measured and reported, whatever the value |
| KR3 | Missed safety escalations are counted separately | Safety routing recall, per language | Measured; target for the agent is 100% |
| KR4 | Every number can be trusted by someone who reads the language | Reviewer agreement rate | Reported for both test languages |
| KR5 | The next language costs less than this one did | A written recommendation on which approach to use, with evidence | Published in the repo |

KR2 sets the bar at having the number, not at the number being good. If the agent turns out to be unreliable in Indonesian and that is measured properly, the project has done its job.

## 5. Who this is for

Three groups, grouped by the problem they have rather than the industry they sit in.

### Teams running one policy across several language markets

They have a controlled document in one language, usually English, and customers who write in others. Their job is to automate support without ever telling a customer something untrue.

This shape appears in e-commerce returns, telco billing disputes, airline fare and change rules, insurance claim eligibility, and banking chargebacks. In every case the policy is a governed document and the customer is not reading it.

**Their constraint:** the policy usually cannot be translated. It is legally controlled, version-managed, and often signed off by a compliance function. Whatever they build has to work with the English original.

### Teams deciding whether a grounding check is enough

They are choosing safety measures for a support agent and have been told that citation checking or grounding prevents confidently wrong answers.

**Their constraint:** they need to know the limits of that claim before they rely on it, not after.

### Teams about to add their first non-English market

They have a working English agent and a decision to make: translate at the edge, or teach the system to read the customer directly.

**Their constraint:** they will pick one and live with it, and rebuilding later is expensive.

## 6. What each group gets

### The job to be done

*"I need to automate support in a language my policy is not written in, without ever telling a customer something untrue."*

### What they gain

**A named failure with a number.** Not "be careful with multilingual", but a specific mechanism: the check compares the answer to the facts, and never compares the facts to the customer. Plus the rate at which it happens.

**A safety gap they can check for today.** Escalation for things like a customer reporting a product caught fire is done by matching English words. In another language, nothing matches and the escalation quietly does not happen. Anyone running an English keyword list against non-English customers can check this in an afternoon once they know to look.

**A decision they do not have to make blind.** Translate at the edge, or read the customer's language directly. Both built, both measured on the same tickets, one recommended with evidence.

### Pains avoided

- Trusting a safety measure past the point where it works
- Discovering the gap through a complaint rather than a metric
- Committing to an architecture and finding out later it was the wrong one

### Where this is different from what already exists

Most published work on multilingual support agents measures answer quality: did the model reason well in Spanish. This measures the **checking layer** instead — whether the safety mechanism still functions when the input language changes.

The distinction matters because the checking layer is what teams point to when asked whether their agent is safe.

## 7. Solution

### 7.1 How it works

The full architecture diagram is in the BRD, §6.1. In short, a ticket passes through five steps:

1. **Route it.** Decide what the ticket is about. Escalate straight to a human if it is a safety or fraud matter.
2. **Look up the order.** Pull the customer's order from the database.
3. **Assemble the facts.** Most come from the order record. One — whether the item is faulty — comes from what the customer wrote.
4. **Propose an answer.** The model picks a rule and proposes an outcome.
5. **Check it.** The gate re-tests the proposal. Pass, and it goes to the customer. Fail, and a human takes over.

**Where language matters.** Steps 1 and 3 read the customer's words. Everything else works from structured data. Those two steps are the only places language enters, and the check in step 5 sits downstream of both.

**The core problem, in one line.** Step 5 checks the answer against the facts from step 3. If step 3 got a fact wrong, step 5 has nothing to compare it against, so it approves the mistake.

### 7.2 Key features

**Language-aware routing.** Routing uses a keyword list matched to the customer's language instead of always using the English one. When nothing matches, it records that it fell back, so these cases can be counted instead of vanishing.

**A fact-reading seam.** Reading whether the item is faulty moves behind a single interface with two implementations: a hand-written keyword list per language, and a model that reads the original message. Both work the same way from the outside, so either can be swapped in without changing anything around it.

The existing agent already handles its policy reasoning this way, so the pattern is one the codebase uses rather than one this project introduces.

**A gold facts file.** A separate file recording what the facts should have been for each test ticket. The running system never sees it. Only the scorer uses it, to compare what the system recorded against what was true.

This file is the only place in the design where the facts get checked against reality. Without it there is no independent view of what was true.

**Two new measures.**

- *Silent fact error rate* — of the answers the gate approved and sent, how many were built on a fact the system got wrong.
- *Safety routing recall* — of the tickets that should have gone to a human for safety reasons, how many did.

**Test tickets in three languages.** The existing 68 tickets get Spanish and Indonesian versions with the same correct answers. Most are machine translated and checked by translating back. At least 8 per language are written by hand to sound like real customers: mixed languages in one sentence, informal, typos.

**A cross-language report.** One command runs every combination and produces one table, with raw counts and a confidence interval on every number.

### 7.3 Technology

- Existing repository, extended. The gate, the policy rules, and the order systems are not modified.
- Existing model provider, called through the interface already in the codebase.
- No new dependencies for the offline path. Version 1 runs with no model calls at all, so the automated tests stay free and fast.
- Everything is pinned: fixed dataset date, temperature zero, model name recorded in each report.

### 7.4 Assumptions

Flagged so they can be challenged.

| # | Assumption | Confidence | How it gets tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Only one fact is read from the customer's message | High. Verified by tracing the code. | Breaks if someone adds another message-derived fact later |
| A2 | The gate will approve answers built on wrong facts | High. The mechanism is visible in the source. | Measured directly in iteration 1 |
| A3 | Machine-translated tickets behave like real customer messages | **Low** | The hand-written subset is reported separately. If the two differ, that is a finding. |
| A4 | A model reads a faulty-item report better than a keyword list | **Unproven** | Measured directly as extractor agreement in iteration 2 |
| A5 | Wrong answers stay at zero in every language | Medium-high, predicted from the code | Measured in iteration 1 |
| A6 | A reviewer is available for Indonesian | Medium | Confirm before starting. Iteration 1 does not depend on it. |

A3 and A4 carry the most weight. If A3 fails, the numbers describe translated text rather than real customers. If A4 fails, there is no reason to build the second iteration at all.

## 8. Release plan

No fixed dates. Three iterations, each producing something usable on its own.

### Version 1 — one language, no model, fully reproducible

**Roughly a week of build.**

Spanish only. Language-aware routing, the fact-reading seam running hand-written keyword lists, the gold facts file, both new measures, confidence intervals, and the Spanish run graded by hand.

**Why this first:** a complete, human-checked result with no model in the loop. It runs offline and free, and the numbers stay reproducible however the models change.

**Known limit:** if the Spanish keyword list is written carefully, the silent fact error rate comes out near zero, and that number reflects the quality of the word list more than anything about multilingual grounding. **Version 1 is a baseline, not a finding, and must not be published as one.**

### Version 2 — Indonesian, and the model reader

**Roughly two weeks.**

Indonesian added. The model reader dropped into the same seam. Hand-written ticket subsets, held-out runs, the extra tier of fault-related tickets, and Indonesian graded by the reviewer.

**Why second:** the fact reader changes here, so this is the first run that tests the mechanism. If the numbers move, the mechanism is confirmed. If they hold, the model reads the customer better than the code suggested, and that result is publishable too.

**Also produces:** how often a hand-written word list and a model disagree about whether an Indonesian customer said their item was broken. Running both extractors over the same tickets gives that number at no extra cost.

### Version 3 — the architecture comparison

**Roughly a week.**

Translate-at-the-edge built and run against the same tickets. Six combinations, one table, and a written recommendation on which approach to use.

**Why last:** the least surprising part, the most reusable, and the safest to drop if time runs out.

### What is not in any version

- Translating the policy document
- Translating replies to customers, beyond counting how often they came back in the wrong language
- Hindi and Thai
- A test set large enough to claim statistical significance

### Still to decide

One open item blocks the start of version 1: how many fault-related tickets to add, and which existing categories to draw them from. The gold facts file cannot be written until this is settled.
